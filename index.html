The headline

Memory is the signal that generalizes

The clearest result: of everything the brain does in a Meta ad's opening, activity in the memory regions most reliably predicts performance from one brand to the next. It isn't the single largest correlation when you pool every ad together — valuation and meaning edge it there — but it's the one that barely fades when tested brand by brand. Switch the view to see why that gap is the whole point.

Valuation and meaning lean on scale; memory doesn't. Their aggregate strength comes largely from the few big-data brands — averaged brand by brand, they roughly halve. Memory barely moves. It works about equally well on every brand, large or small, which is exactly what makes it the signal you'd trust on the next brand you'd never seen.

Is it real?

Stronger than randomness — in both directions

Is any of this more than luck? We shuffled each brand's ad performance at random hundreds of times and re-measured — which tells us exactly what correlation chance produces on this data: about ±0.14. Every system clears it (p ≤ .003) — the three that mark better ads, and the two that mark worse ones.

The two negative signals are as sensible as the positives: openings that flood the viewer with auditory load, or demand mental effort to parse, track worse. And the whole signal lives in the first four seconds — average it over the full clip and it washes out entirely.

How we ran it

The method, briefly

TRIBE takes an ad's video, audio, and transcript and predicts the fMRI response an average brain would produce watching it — region by region, moment by moment. We turned that into features and tested whether early-window activation tracks performance.

Performance, fairly. Each Meta ad's ROAS, converted to a percentile within its own brand — every account's ads span 0 to 1, so we only ever ask "better than this brand's other ads," never brand vs brand.

The features. Average predicted activation per region, within-brand centered, measured over the early window — roughly the first four seconds, where the signal turned out to live.

The honest test. Leave-one-account-out: train on 12 brands, predict the 13th, unseen. A pattern only counts if it survives on a brand held out of training.

The baseline — what chance explains. Instead of an arbitrary cutoff, we shuffled each brand's ad performance at random hundreds of times and re-measured. That reveals exactly what correlation randomness produces on this data — about ±0.14. A real signal has to beat that noise floor. We use the strict "two-sided" test (a fluke in either direction counts against us), and every system still clears it at p ≤ .003.

What it suggests for creative

Insights for the next ad

This isn't a score to gate creative on — the effect is real but modest, and built on 13 brands. Treat it as a set of testable hypotheses about what the opening seconds should do, each one grounded in where the brain signal actually pointed.

  1. Win the first four seconds — that's where it's decided

    The signal exists early and vanishes when averaged over the whole clip. Whatever the opening does — land a meaning, spark a want, lodge a memory — is what tracked performance. The back half of the ad barely registered.

  2. Make it memorable before you make it pretty

    Memory encoding was the most reliable signal of all, brand to brand. The question worth asking of an opening isn't only "is this appealing?" but "will they still have this in mind three seconds from now?" A distinct, sticky idea up front beats a polished but forgettable one.

  3. Let the meaning land fast

    Semantic regions — the brain grasping what this is about — carried real signal. An opening whose point registers immediately outperforms one the viewer has to decode. Clarity of idea in second one, not a slow build.

  4. Keep the opening easy — don't make them work

    The regions that tracked worse ads were early auditory load and effortful cognitive control. Openings that are sonically busy or demand mental effort to parse read as friction. The best openings feel effortless, not loud.

  5. Use it as a lens, not a verdict

    These are directional priors for critiquing an opening or shaping a test — "does this land a memorable meaning in the first beats?" — not a number that greenlights or kills an ad. The value is in the questions it sharpens.

Standing back

Where this lands

Early-window activation in the brain's meaning, valuation, and memory regions predicts within-brand Meta ad performance about twice as strongly as chance allows — a real, statistically robust signal of modest size, and most consistent, brand to brand, in the memory regions. The open questions now are magnitude and scale, not whether it exists.

Limits to hold in mind
  • 13 accounts is the binding ceiling; per-brand estimates rest on 7–22 ads and carry wide margins.
  • The effect is real but modest — it explains a meaningful slice of within-brand variance, not most of it.
  • TRIBE gives predicted, not measured, brain response — and cortical only. Subcortical reward (the "buy button") wasn't tested.
  • Everything here is correlational, and time windows are approximate.
What would sharpen it
  • More brands — the one move that changes the answer — then re-run the held-out test.
  • Isolate the memory signal further, since it's the one that travels between brands.
  • Test the creative hypotheses above directly, in live A/B splits.
